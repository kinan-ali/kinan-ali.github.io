Adaptive Control for Stewart Platform using Neural Networks

My graduation project at HIAST. I investigated how Neural Networks can learn to control a 6-DOF robot in real-time, compensating for mechanical imperfections that traditional PID controllers can't see.

🚀 Current Status: I am currently preparing a research paper detailing the novel adaptive laws developed for this system. As such, the specific mathematical derivations are omitted here, but enjoy the results below!

Step 1: The Mechanical "Upgrade"

The standard platform wasn't enough. I needed more range. I modified the mechanics, installing powerful servos and designing longer arms to expand the workspace.

Of course, I didn't just guess—I validated the new workspace in MATLAB/Simulink first.

Step 2: Giving the Robot "Eyes"

To control the platform, I needed to know exactly where it was. My first attempt involved a standard webcam and two ArUco markers.

The Result? Chaos. The camera noise was too high, introducing jitter into the control loop. I needed a cleaner signal.

The Fix: I switched to a high-resolution mobile camera stream over WiFi and replaced the single markers with a full ArUco Board. Using the Perspective-N-Point (PnP) algorithm, I achieved stable, sub-millimeter pose estimation.

Lesson learned: Better data beats better algorithms every time.

Step 3: The "Fun" Part

With the sensors calibrated and the Neural Network trained to cancel out the motor backlash, it was time for the ultimate test.

Can a Neural Network play Ping Pong?

Control Board

Moving from high-level Python AI to the gritty world of embedded hardware. Designed a universal PCB for washing machine automation.

While robotics is about abstract math, this project was about reliability. I designed a custom PCB based on the ATmega16 microcontroller to replace aging mechanical timers in industrial machines.

Key Engineering Challenges:

  • Power Control: Implemented TRIAC-triggered control for high-voltage motors.
  • PID on 8-bit: wrote a PID speed controller in C from scratch (no fancy libraries!).
  • Simulation: Fully validated the logic in Proteus before manufacturing.

This project taught me that in the industrial world, code doesn't just need to work—it needs to survive voltage spikes and EMI.

Data-Driven vs Classical Control
3RRR Parallel Manipulator

A battle between traditional control theory and modern data-driven approaches. Who wins?

The Premise: We know how to control robots when we have a perfect mathematical model (Mass, Length, Inertia). But what if the model is wrong?

1. The Theoretical Setup

Before writing code, I had to understand the robot's limits. I performed a full Singularity Analysis to find the "danger zones" where the robot loses control.

Using these equations, I mapped out the safe workspace (shown below), ensuring my path planning algorithms would never force the robot into a locked position.

2. The Controller

I designed a Neural Network to learn the Inverse Kinematics. The idea is simple: instead of telling the robot "move arm A by 10 degrees," we ask the network "what motors should I move to get to position X?"

3. The Results

I tested the controller on a physical prototype. The results (below) showed that the Data-Driven method could track the reference trajectory almost as well as the classical method, but without needing any physical measurements of link lengths.

This project sparked my interest in Contraction Theory: How can we mathematically guarantee that these learned controllers won't go unstable?

Flyback DC-DC Converter Design

A power electronics workshop at HIAST. A lesson in "Optimized Engineering."

This project wasn't just about making 24V from 12V. It was about doing it safely and cheaply.

1. Design & Simulation

We chose the Discontinuous Current Mode (DCM) to simplify the control loop. I modeled the entire circuit in MATLAB and PSpice first.

2. Component Optimization (The Engineering Part)

Anyone can build a converter if they have infinite budget. The challenge was finding the minimal safe components.

  • The Transistor Dilemma: My calculations showed the voltage spike ($V_{ds}$) could reach 105V. To save cost, I chose the IRF3710 (rated for 100V). It was "living on the edge," but testing showed the real spikes were manageable with a good Snubber circuit.
  • The "Magic Smoke" Moment: Initially, I used a standard diode for the Snubber circuit. It was too slow! The voltage spikes shot up to 120V on the scope. I swapped it for a fast 1N4148, and the spikes vanished immediately.
  • The Transformer: I wound it myself—7 turns on the primary, 10 on the secondary—calculating the exact air gap to prevent saturation.
